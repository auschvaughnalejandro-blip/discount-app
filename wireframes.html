Wireframes / Draft 2 — revised scope

Privilege Guest Program

Screen definitions for the member app, the staff verification page, and the admin dashboard. Layout and hierarchy only — no visual design. Numbered notes carry the reasoning.

Property Steigenberger Hotel Doha Surfaces Member app · Verification · Dashboard Screens 14

What this draft removes

The client has confirmed a single-property programme with a fixed benefit schedule and no points. Removed from draft 1: the points ledger, tiers, earn rates, the segment and targeting engine, competitive tenant isolation, POS integration, and the WhatsApp campaign layer.

What remains: show members their benefits, let staff verify and record a redemption, and let the hotel see who used what.

1 · Claim membership

The programme is invitation-only, so onboarding runs backwards from a normal app — the account exists before the member does.

  1. No public signup. An administrator creates the member and issues a code. There is no route into the programme from this screen without one.
  2. The claim code is single-use. The membership number appears on both the printed card and the letter — a reusable code would let anyone holding a discarded letter activate a membership.
  3. Phone number captured here because it becomes the sign-in credential from this point on.
  4. Exclusivity stated plainly. It is the programme's defining characteristic and the first screen should feel like it.

2 · Verify & consent

Confirms the phone number, checks pre-filled details, and captures communication consent.

  1. The name is pre-filled, not asked. An administrator already entered it when creating the membership. The member confirms rather than types.
  2. Consent is per channel and unticked by default. Qatar's data protection law requires explicit prior consent specifically for direct electronic marketing — bundling it into activation would invalidate it.
  3. The printed benefits sheet already promises notification of changes by the member's preferred channel, so this is a programme commitment, not only a marketing preference.
  4. One-time codes are hashed, single-use, and expire in minutes, with attempt limits per number.

3 · Explore

The landing screen. Replaces the printed benefits sheet as the thing a member glances at.

  1. Membership number is prominent. It is the member's identity in the programme, printed on their physical card, and the thing staff ask for.
  2. Five categories, matching the printed sheet exactly. Members already know this structure; changing it would create confusion for no gain.
  3. The headline discount sits on each tile. The percentage is what people actually want to recall.
  4. Member events surfaced here. The invitation letter promises invitations to exclusive gatherings, but the benefits table omits them — worth confirming whether they belong in the app.
  5. No points, no balance, no progress bar. Nothing is earned, so there is nothing to track. Benefits are permanent from the day of joining.

4 · Offers

The full benefit list. A direct digital replacement for the printed sheet.

  1. Everything visible without tapping. The paper sheet showed all five at once; hiding terms behind a tap would be a downgrade.
  2. Party-size limits shown on the list, not buried. "Maximum 6 guests" is precisely the detail that causes an awkward conversation at a restaurant if the member did not know it.
  3. Identical for every member. One membership level, no personalization — confirmed by the client as "just show them the list of all offers."
  4. Content comes from the dashboard. The printed sheet states benefits may change without notice, so nothing here can be hardcoded.

6 · Search

Text search across benefits, outlets and terms.

  1. Specified by the client, though with five categories it is a convenience rather than a necessity. It is inexpensive, so it stays.
  2. Searches inside terms, not only titles. "Kids" and "parking" are the queries people will actually type, and neither is a category name.
  3. Suggested chips give it purpose when empty, which is most of the time on a list this short.
  4. Worth revisiting if the benefit list grows. At five categories it earns its place mainly as a habit users bring from other apps.

7 · Digital card

The credential. A digital equivalent of the printed card, reachable from anywhere in the app.

  1. The code identifies; it does not authorise. It carries an opaque member reference — staff confirm entitlement against the member record before applying any discount.
  2. The payload rotates. These are high-value benefits — 40% off spa, 30% off rooms — so a static code that could be screenshotted and forwarded would be worth real money. A stale payload fails verification.
  3. Name and number mirror the physical card, so staff recognise it instantly and the two are interchangeable.
  4. The physical card is not replaced. This supplements it for members who do not have it with them.

8 · Profile

Identity, history, and settings.

  1. Redemption history is the substance of this screen. It answers "have I already used the spa discount this month" without a phone call, and gives the member their own record of the programme's value.
  2. Party size is recorded and shown, because two benefits carry hard caps — six guests for dining, two for spa.
  3. This is the same data the hotel sees. Members viewing their own history is a transparency benefit and reduces disputes.
  4. Notification preferences are editable here, and withdrawing consent must take effect immediately across every channel.

9 · Verify

The default screen at a reception desk or restaurant host stand.

  1. A web page, not an app. Staff open a URL on a device already behind the counter. No installation, no procurement, no app store — which is what keeps this within the client's stated scope.
  2. Both entry paths supported. Many members will present the printed card rather than the app, so typing the number must work as well as scanning.
  3. Staff sign in individually. Every redemption is attributed to a named person — without that, an incorrectly applied 40% discount is untraceable.
  4. No search, no member list. Staff can retrieve only the person in front of them. Given who these members are, that restriction is deliberate.

10 · Member found

Confirms entitlement and records the redemption. This is where the dashboard's data comes from.

  1. Validity is stated before anything else. A suspended or expired membership must be obvious at a glance, before staff begin applying a discount.
  2. Party size is a required field, not a note. Two benefits carry hard caps, and without it the limits are unenforceable and reporting is incomplete.
  3. Bill amount is optional but valuable. It is the only route to knowing what the programme costs — worth confirming whether staff will realistically enter it.
  4. The system records; it does not discount. The discount is applied on the hotel's existing till exactly as today. We are adding a record, not replacing a payment process.
  5. Recent use is shown to staff, so a member exceeding a reasonable pattern is visible at the counter rather than discovered in a report months later.

11 · Members

The list the client explicitly asked for: every member, their ID, and what they have used.

  1. "New member" replaces public signup. An administrator creates the record and issues a claim code — this is how someone joins an invitation-only programme.
  2. Dormant members are surfaced, not buried. Someone invited personally by the General Manager who has never used a benefit is a relationship problem, and it is exactly what the programme currently cannot see at all.
  3. Export is administrator-only and individually logged. This is a list of named, prominent individuals — bulk export is the most sensitive action in the system.
  4. Small by design. The reference card is PG-0003. This is a list of dozens, not thousands, which is why it can be a simple table rather than an analytics product.

14 · Benefit management

Where the printed benefits sheet becomes editable. This is the heart of "backend access."

  1. The test of whether this project delivered. Changing the F&B discount from 25% to 20% must be a form field. If it needs a developer and a release, the client does not have backend access regardless of how good the rest looks.
  2. Rules stored as data, read at runtime. Discounts, caps, phone numbers and terms are all configuration — more work upfront, and the difference between a product they operate and one they depend on us to change.
  3. Changes are versioned and attributed. "Who changed the spa discount, and when" is a question that will be asked.
  4. Publishing triggers member notification, honouring the commitment already printed on the benefits sheet — and only to members who consented to that channel.
04

Desktop layouts

Same product, different constraints

How desktop differs

Desktop is not a wider phone. Two structural changes drive everything below: navigation becomes persistent rather than a bottom bar, and list and detail can sit side by side instead of being separate screens you move between.

The member app is mobile-first — it is used standing at a restaurant. The web version exists for members browsing at leisure and is a genuine convenience, not the primary surface. The admin dashboard is the reverse: desktop-first, because nobody manages a membership list on a phone.

D1 · Member web — Explore

The same content as the mobile Explore screen, using the extra width to remove navigation steps rather than to add features.

  1. All five benefits visible at once with their headline percentages. On mobile this needs two rows and a scroll; here it is a single glance, which is closer to the printed sheet it replaces.
  2. The card is permanently visible, not behind a tap. There is room for it, so there is no reason to hide it.
  3. Reservation numbers listed together in a sidebar panel. Tap-to-call is meaningless on a desktop, so the numbers are simply shown — the same information adapted to the device rather than ported unchanged.
  4. Navigation moves to the top. A bottom tab bar is a touch convention and looks wrong on a pointer device.
  5. Membership number sits in the header, always present, because it is the member's identity and the thing staff ask for on the phone.
  6. No new functionality. Everything here exists on mobile. Desktop earns its place by reducing navigation, not by expanding scope.

D3 · Admin — Members

The full desktop shell. Persistent sidebar, wide table, everything reachable without a menu.

  1. Sidebar navigation is always present. Administrators move between members, redemptions and benefits constantly; hiding that behind a menu costs a click every time.
  2. The table gains four columns over mobile — total uses, most-used benefit, app status, membership status. Scanning a list of dozens is exactly what a wide screen is good at.
  3. "Not claimed" is its own signal. A member who was issued a card but never activated the app is a different problem from one who activated it and never visits — and the two need different follow-up.
  4. Sorting and filtering replace the mobile summary card. On a small screen you surface one insight; here the administrator can find it themselves.
  5. Export stays in the header but remains administrator-only and individually logged. Its prominence must not imply it is routine.
  6. The signed-in identity is always visible in the sidebar. On a shared back-office machine, knowing whose session is open matters.

D4 · Admin — Member detail

One member, in full. The most sensitive screen in the system.

  1. Contact details are masked by default. Revealing a phone number should be a deliberate action, logged like any other. Most tasks on this screen do not require seeing it.
  2. Every history row names the staff member who recorded it. Attribution makes a wrongly applied 40% discount traceable, and it is the main deterrent against misuse.

  6. Suspend, never delete. Deleting a member destroys the redemption history the reporting depends on.

D6 · Verification — front desk

The same page as on a tablet, laid out for a reception workstation.

  1. Scanning stays permanently available in the sidebar rather than being a separate screen. At a desk, the next guest arrives while the previous record is still open.
  2. Member details and the recording form sit side by side. Staff confirm identity and record the benefit without scrolling, which matters when someone is standing in front of them.
  3. Still a web page, not an application. It runs on the machine already at the desk — no installation, no procurement, which is what keeps this inside the client's stated scope.
  4. No member list, no name search, here or anywhere. Staff retrieve only the person in front of them, by exact number or scan. Given who these members are, that restriction is the point rather than a detail.

Responsive strategy

Member experience — mobile first. Built for the phone, then widened. Above roughly 900px the bottom tab bar becomes a top navigation, benefit lists move from one column to three, and list-and-detail split into two panes. Below that it is the mobile layout unchanged.

Admin dashboard — desktop first. Built for a wide screen. Below roughly 900px the sidebar collapses to a menu, wide tables reduce to the three most important columns, and multi-column report grids stack. It remains usable on a phone for looking something up, but it is not designed for management on one.

Verification page — works at both. It must run on a tablet at a restaurant host stand and on a workstation at reception. This is the one surface where neither size is secondary.

Arabic and right-to-left affect every layout on this page — sidebar position, table column order, and text alignment all mirror. Plan it from the start rather than retrofitting.

Notes

  • No points, tiers or balances anywhere. Benefits are a fixed schedule granted on joining. Every progress bar, balance and tier ladder from the previous draft has been removed.
  • The verification page is not a fourth product. It is a page within the dashboard, opened on whatever device is already at the counter.
  • Visual design is absent by intention. These fix hierarchy and flow. Given the programme's positioning, the eventual design needs to be considerably more restrained and premium than a typical loyalty app.
  • Arabic and right-to-left layout affect every screen and should be planned from the start.
  • Open before build: what the existing QR code does, whether staff will record bill amounts, and whether priority reservations and member events belong in the app.
